# Evidence brief: misperceived norms about women's work

For the "forecast the norm" loop in [Nivel play and markets](../plans/nivel-play-and-markets.md) and the MIT Solve application ([mit-solve-application.md](../plans/mit-solve-application.md)). The sources for resolving questions are in [resolution-sources.md](resolution-sources.md); measurement is in [markets-evaluation.md](../plans/markets-evaluation.md).

## 1. What the evidence shows

**People underestimate how many others support women working.**
- In Saudi Arabia, 87% of young married men in the experiment agreed that women should be allowed to work outside the home. About three-quarters underestimated how many similar men agreed.
- In a national sample, 82% agreed, and 92% underestimated others' support.
- Source: Bursztyn, González and Yanagizawa-Drott (2020), "Misperceived Social Norms: Women Working Outside the Home in Saudi Arabia", *American Economic Review*. Summary: [Becker Friedman Institute](https://bfi.uchicago.edu/insight/research-summary/misperceived-social-norms-female-labor-force-participation-in-saudi-arabia).

**Correcting that belief changed what people did, in that study.**
- Men told the true share signed their wives up for a job-matching service more often: 32% against 23% in the control group.
- Three to five months later, their wives were 10 percentage points more likely to have applied for a job, and 5 points more likely to have interviewed for one.
- Women told the true level of support switched from an at-home job to a better-paid job outside the home.

**The pattern holds across countries, with a twist.**
- Newly collected, nationally representative data from 60 countries show misperceptions of gender norms everywhere.
- In less gender-equal countries, people underestimate support for women working and for affirmative action, especially support among men.
- In more gender-equal countries, people overestimate support for affirmative action and underestimate support for basic rights.
- Three forces drive it: false consensus, overweighting the minority view, and gender stereotyping.
- Source: Bursztyn, Cappelen, Tungodden, Voena and Yanagizawa-Drott, ["How Are Gender Norms Perceived?"](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31049), NBER Working Paper 31049 (revised November 2024). India's own figures are in the paper's country results; read them before quoting a number for India.

**In India, the barrier is real, and a light touch is not enough.**
- Of women outside the labour force, 44.4% named child care or running the home as the main reason (Periodic Labour Force Survey, Annual Report 2025, press note of 27 March 2026).
- Two light-touch, employer-led interventions in Karnataka found no meaningful change in support for women's work or in women's employment: an orientation video for families, and a guided conversation between a woman and her family. Source: Dean and Jayachandran, [J-PAL evaluation summary](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/impact-light-touch-employer-led-interventions-attitudes-around-womens-work-india).

## 2. What this means for Nivel

- **The mechanism Nivel can test** is a correction of second-order beliefs: what people think others believe. Its markets and the game expose people to the true figure, repeatedly, from a named source.
- **What Nivel cannot claim:**
  - That forecasting changes behaviour. The Saudi study measured behaviour, and Nivel does not.
  - That one exposure shifts a norm. The Karnataka result is the warning.
- **Claims stay at stated beliefs.** "After resolution, players' estimates of what others believe moved closer to the survey figure" is a claim the design can support ([markets-evaluation.md](../plans/markets-evaluation.md)).
- **Repetition is the design answer** to the Karnataka result: markets resolve on a schedule, and players come back at each resolution and each game night.

## 3. What to forecast, and what not to

**Ask:**
- **The data:**
  - Nivel's next published figures, at index level, such as the Nifty 50's women's share of pay next year.
  - Official statistics with dated releases, such as women's labour force participation in a named PLFS bulletin.
- **The norm:**
  - The share of a named population that gave a stated answer to a published survey question.
  - The exact wording, sample and year are shown before anyone forecasts.
- **Your own bias:** after resolution, each player's estimates against the results, by topic ([PARITY-50](https://app.plane.so/claude-pri/projects/00987666-dbc9-4caa-8c8d-004db87e6c11/issues/8c45b074-94e8-4151-b8ad-2ba57a6e145d)).

**Do not ask:**
- **About a named person, or a single small company.** Index and population level only, as in the Spot feed.
- **Whether a view is right.** No "should" questions: players forecast what people say, never what is good.
- **About violence, harassment or abuse,** even where a survey asks (for example NFHS questions on attitudes to wife-beating). Turning them into a game trivialises them, and the play side's child-safety rules apply in spirit.
- **Splits by religion or caste.** They invite ranking groups against each other, which the markets cannot moderate.
- **Questions with no named, dated publication to resolve against.** A survey wave with no release date gets no market until it has one.
- **Questions a player could move:** none about a single employer's own staff figures.

## 4. Wording: describe beliefs, never endorse them

1. **Quote the survey.** Give the question's own wording, the population, the year and the source, and link it.
2. **Say who answered.** "What share of men aged 18 to 49 in the survey said…", never "do people think…".
3. **No loaded words.** Not "wrongly", "still", "backward" or "progressive". Neutral verbs only: said, agreed, answered.
4. **Same frame both ways.** Where a survey asks men and women, offer both questions, so neither group is cast as the problem.
5. **A number out of 100,** as in the game: the share who gave the answer.
6. **"Don't know" is shown as the survey reports it,** never folded into a side.
7. **British spelling, readable in Indian English, no em dashes.** The same house rules as the Spot feed ([plan section 4.3](../plans/spot-the-stereotype-anytime-and-nivel.md#43-writing-rules-for-nivel-items)).
8. **Translation** keeps the survey's own translated wording where one exists, and is back-translated where it does not.

## 5. Open points

- **India's figures in the 60-country study:** read them from the paper before using any.
- **Second-order belief questions in Indian surveys** (what people think others believe) are rare. Most norm markets will resolve on first-order shares, with the player's guess as the second-order belief.
